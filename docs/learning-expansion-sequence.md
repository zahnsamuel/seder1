# Learning expansion sequence

## 1. Make the first two units durable

Create 3–5 review variants for every skill in Berakhot Units 1 and 2. Variants must use a different source context, wording, or distractor pattern so a learner demonstrates transfer rather than recall of a prior screen.

## 2. Complete the beginner Gemara runway

Add units on Mishnah grammar, core Hebrew/Aramaic connectors, baraitot, objections, distinctions, and conclusions. Each unit ends with an unfamiliar short source that learners annotate by function before opening translation.

## 3. Build tractate arcs, not isolated labs

For each selected tractate, create an entry lesson, three to five connected source sequences, a vocabulary set, recurring retrieval, and an independent checkpoint. Prioritize Berakhot, Shabbat, Eruvin, Pesachim, Bava Metzia, and Sukkah before widening further.

## 4. Deepen Jewish Thought by questions

Organize units around durable questions: knowing God, revelation and reason, freedom and providence, ethics and character, interpretation, prayer, suffering, peoplehood, and modernity. Each question should include multiple Jewish voices, a clear conceptual disagreement, and a reflection that does not pretend to issue a ruling.

## 5. Build cross-track connections carefully

Connect a Gemara source to Thought only when the relationship is explicit: a legal category raises an ethical or metaphysical question; a philosophical concept clarifies a value without deciding law. Label the connection so learners know whether they are studying legal argument, conceptual interpretation, or both.

## 6. Teach Judaism and the Wider World with precision

Begin every comparison with a Jewish primary source and name the historical setting of both traditions. Do not reduce Western, Eastern, Islamic, Christian, Greek, Roman, Persian, Indian, or East Asian canons to a single “outside perspective.” Learners should distinguish analogy, historical influence, translation, disagreement, and shared human question. Law and philosophy belong in the same comparative track only when the connection is explicit: for example, how legal traditions meet commerce, medicine, public institutions, migration, or modern rights language; or how thinkers address reason, revelation, ethics, and human flourishing.
